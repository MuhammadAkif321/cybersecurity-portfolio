# Module 2 Summary: Security Frameworks and Controls

## 1. Security frameworks

A **security framework** is a structured set of guidelines, standards, and best practices that helps an organization plan, manage, and improve its cybersecurity.

Frameworks give organizations a consistent way to understand risks, choose security measures, and protect important information and systems. They help guide security work rather than leaving every decision to guesswork.

## 2. Security controls

**Security controls** are safeguards that reduce security risks and help protect an organization's assets.

Examples include:
- Firewalls that filter network traffic
- Encryption that makes data unreadable to unauthorized people
- Access permissions that limit who can view or change information
- Security policies and employee training
- Cameras, locks, and other physical protections

Controls can be administrative, technical, or physical. They may also be designed to prevent incidents, detect them, correct their effects, or discourage unwanted actions.

A control being present does not automatically mean it is strong or effective. For example, an organization may have a password policy, but the policy could still allow weak passwords.

## 3. The CIA triad

The **CIA triad** is a basic model used to think about the security of information and systems.

- **Confidentiality:** Only authorized people can access information. Example: customer records are available only to employees who need them.
- **Integrity:** Information remains accurate, complete, and trustworthy. Example: an unauthorized person cannot secretly change payment records.
- **Availability:** Authorized users can access information and services when needed. Example: a business restores its systems after an outage so staff can continue working.

Security decisions should consider all three parts. A measure that protects confidentiality should not unnecessarily prevent authorized users from accessing the information they need.

## 4. NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF)

The **NIST Cybersecurity Framework** provides guidance for managing and reducing cybersecurity risk. Its six core functions are:

1. **Govern:** Establish and oversee the organization's cybersecurity strategy, expectations, policies, and responsibilities.
2. **Identify:** Understand the organization's assets, systems, risks, and the impact of cybersecurity threats.
3. **Protect:** Put safeguards in place to reduce the likelihood or impact of incidents.
4. **Detect:** Monitor systems and identify possible cybersecurity events quickly.
5. **Respond:** Take action to contain, investigate, and manage a cybersecurity incident.
6. **Recover:** Restore affected systems and services and improve recovery capabilities.

Together, these functions help an organization manage cybersecurity before, during, and after an incident.

## 5. NIST SP 800-53

**NIST Special Publication 800-53** provides a catalog of security and privacy controls for information systems and organizations. It is especially relevant to U.S. federal information systems, and its controls can help organizations select safeguards based on their needs and risks.

It is important to distinguish it from the NIST CSF: the CSF gives high-level cybersecurity risk-management outcomes, while SP 800-53 provides a detailed catalog of controls.

## 6. OWASP and secure design

The **Open Worldwide Application Security Project (OWASP)** is a nonprofit community focused on improving software and application security.

Secure design means considering security from the beginning of software development, not waiting until an application is finished. Applying secure design principles helps reduce weaknesses that attackers could exploit. Examples include limiting access to only what is needed, using secure defaults, and validating input.

## 7. Security audits

A **security audit** is a review of an organization's security controls, policies, and procedures against defined expectations.

Important audit elements include:

- **Scope:** Defines what the audit will cover, such as people, assets, systems, policies, and procedures.
- **Goals:** Explain what the organization wants the audit to achieve.
- **Assets:** Identify items of value that need protection.
- **Control assessment:** Review whether safeguards exist and whether they address the risks.
- **Findings and recommendations:** Record gaps and suggest improvements.

An audit helps an organization understand its security posture and decide where improvements are needed.

## Key takeaway

Security frameworks provide direction, controls provide safeguards, secure design helps build safer applications, and audits help evaluate whether security practices meet expectations. These concepts work together to protect an organization's data, systems, people, and operations.
